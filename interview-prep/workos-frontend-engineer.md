## STAR scenarios

Two stories ready, each ~60–90 seconds spoken. Use them when she asks about ownership, customer impact, or accessibility specifically. Don't deliver both back-to-back — pick the one that fits the question.

### Owning projects + driving work based on customer impact (Gitwit DHS)

**Situation/Task:**
> "At Gitwit during the pandemic, we spun up a child-care benefits platform — DHS-funded — that let essential workers pay family members already in their pods so they could keep showing up to work. There was a base design, but we were adding new features and I led the frontend rewrite. The audience was the constraint: nurses, EMTs, social workers, often applying after a long shift, sometimes on their own phone but often on a hospital lobby terminal or a social-services office machine. We couldn't assume modern browsers, fast connections, *or* private devices."

**Action:**
> "A few things. I stayed conservative on bundle size and modern browser features because that's what the deployment context actually demanded. Session and form-state handling had to assume the next person at the device wasn't the user — no PII cached, no sticky autofill on a public terminal. And I gave a company-wide WCAG talk during the build, not after — because the team was adding new components and we needed a shared accessibility bar so the new work didn't undo the floor we already had."

**Result:**
> "The platform ran at $2M a year, serving essential workers paying their family-pod caregivers. It held up under the actual usage pattern, which was the harder bar than scale."

**Reflection (the WorkOS hook):**
> "That project locked in something that's stayed with me — design to the user's *actual* context, not the abstracted persona. The dated equipment and the public terminals weren't edge cases; they were the spec. WorkOS's customers are developers, but the same instinct applies: who's actually at the keyboard, in what mode, with what budget? Radix's whole proposition is 'correct-by-default a11y so the consumer can compose freely,' and that maps directly to what I learned at Gitwit — give people a floor that holds even when conditions are hostile."

### Understanding common a11y patterns (MSU RCPD)

**Situation/Task:**
> "At Gravity Works, I led the frontend on a full a11y rebuild of MSU's Resource Center for Persons with Disabilities site. The audience was the spec — students relying on the site to navigate disability services on campus. I tag-teamed with another dev, and we tested throughout the build with a visually-impaired user, because emulators only get you so far. The trickiest single piece was the megamenu, but the real work was re-authoring the a11y layer across the whole site — full keyboard navigation, autoplay banner video that didn't violate motion-reduction preferences, all of it."

**Action:**
> "We were handed visual designs and a stakeholder contact, but no spec for keyboard interactions or ARIA roles — so I derived those myself from the WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices. Tab-index management, focus traps for modals, escape-to-close, arrow-key navigation in the megamenu, focus restoration when overlays closed. Modal placement in the page hierarchy so screen readers could find them. Reduced-motion media queries on the banner video. Each pattern got run by our user-tester, and we kept what held up under real use."

**Result:**
> "Site shipped, RCPD signed off, and the nav layer worked for a screen-reader user without us being there to demo it."

**Reflection (the WorkOS hook):**
> "That was the muscle I built earliest — *deriving* a11y semantics from first principles and the WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices, on a project with no library to lean on. Radix Primitives are essentially that muscle, productized. The work I had to do by hand for that megamenu is the work Radix gives you for free, so consumers can compose freely on top. That's why the Radix philosophy reads so naturally to me — it's the codified version of work I've already had to do."
